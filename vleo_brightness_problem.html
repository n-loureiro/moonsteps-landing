This note turns "make very-low-orbit satellites dimmer" into a concrete engineering problem. It covers sunlight reflected by satellites at altitudes of roughly 250–450 km (VLEO), as seen by observers on the ground. Radio interference and thermal infrared emission are out of scope, except where they act as constraints.

1. Target: what counts as dark enough

The IAU Centre for the Protection of the Dark and Quiet Sky (CPS) recommends that satellites in operational orbits stay invisible to the naked eye.11 Eggl, Peel, Rawls, Walker & IAU CPS, Proc. 9th ESA Space Debris Conf., 2025, §4.1. The derivation is in Boley et al. 2025, RNAAS 9, 60. Above 550 km the limit gets stricter with altitude:

V>{7+2.5log10h550 kmfor h>550 km7for h≤550 km

All of VLEO falls in the flat V > 7 regime. Separate per-band limits, derived from what the Vera C. Rubin Observatory can tolerate, apply in narrow filters:

The same recommendations also ask operators to minimise flares and glints, publish a brightness model based on how each surface reflects light (its BRDF) and check it against on-orbit observations, keep satellites dim during orbit raising and deorbit, and not hide the energy by re-emitting it in the 3–4 µm or 8–14 µm infrared windows.

2. The observable

Model the satellite as flat facets k. Each facet has an area Ak, a normal n^k in the body frame, and a reflectance fk (its BRDF) that changes over time as atomic oxygen and UV age the materials. s^ is the unit vector to the Sun and o^ the unit vector to the observer, both in the body frame. d is the distance to the observer (slant range), and visk accounts for self-shadowing and occlusion.

σ(s^,o^)=∑kAkfk(s^,o^;t)(n^k·s^)+(n^k·o^)+visk [m²/sr] V=Vsun−2.5log10(σd2), Vsun=−26.74

For comparison, a single 1 m² white plate that scatters light evenly in all directions (Lambertian, ρ ≈ 0.9) gives σ ≈ 0.3 m²/sr at favourable geometry, about 100× over budget at 300 km. The magnitude limit is flat below 550 km, but a closer satellite looks brighter by 5log10(h/550) mag (−1.3 mag at 300 km). So a VLEO satellite needs about 3.4× less σ than the same satellite would need at 550 km.

3. Where the constraint actually binds

A satellite is sunlit only if |r^·s^|<sinβ, with

β(h)=arccos(R⊕R⊕+h)

This assumes a cylindrical Earth shadow and ignores the atmosphere, which in reality makes the shadow somewhat wider.

Three things follow from this geometry.

Visibility shrinks to twilight and low elevations. A satellite directly overhead is sunlit only while the Sun is less than β below the observer's horizon. Below about 328 km, β is under 18°, so satellites overhead are in Earth's shadow throughout astronomical night. The cases that matter are twilight, low passes toward the Sun's direction (where the larger distance helps), and high-latitude summer nights.

The Earth-facing side is always lit in exactly the case that matters, at grazing incidence. If an observer near the point directly below the satellite is in darkness, then r^·s^<0. That means sunlight hits the nadir face (normal −r^) at a grazing angle between 0 and β(h), at most 17° at 300 km. The antenna must face the Earth, so this side can't be turned away from observers. The only levers left are how the surface reflects at grazing angles and whether other parts of the satellite can shade it. At grazing angles, coatings tend to become mirror-like (Fresnel reflection) and reflectance data is weakest.

Trails are fast and out of focus for large telescopes. Both effects lower the peak brightness per pixel. They partly offset the extra brightness from being closer, but only for survey telescopes, not for naked-eye viewing or the cultural value of the night sky.

4. The sizing scenario for BRDF evaluation

The problem comes down to orbits, attitudes and altitudes. Together with the set of observers who count, they decide which illumination and viewing angles the satellite's surfaces are ever seen at. A material only needs low reflectance at those angles. The practical task is therefore to find the sizing scenario: the geometry that drives the design, and the region of each material's BRDF that has to be measured and minimised to keep the satellite fainter than V = 7.

For the face that can't be hidden, the Earth-facing side of a nadir-pointing satellite, the scenario can be computed directly. The observer set uses the values in the Airbus–Eutelsat requirements for ESA (Section 5). Night-time starts when the Sun is 12° below the horizon (nautical twilight; astronomical modes use 18°). Telescopes point as low as +20° for near-Earth-object surveys such as Catalina, and at +45° for most other programmes. The computation sweeps every Sun position on the sunlit side of the shadow edge and every observer who sees the satellite at least 20° up while their Sun is at least 12° down. It records the angles at which the face is seen.22 Computed for this note; the script is in vleo_brightness_build.py. Atmospheric refraction and extinction are ignored.

Four results follow.

The peak is always the overhead pass at the shadow edge. For a matte face, the brightest moment is with the satellite overhead and the Sun just above the edge of Earth's shadow (β below the observer's horizon). This is the moment Figure 1 animates. Lower orbits give the brighter peak: V 3.2 at 300 km versus V 4.2 at 550 km for the same 10 m² face.

Sunlight always arrives at grazing angles. At 300 km, sunlight meets the face 6–17° above its surface, never more steeply. At 550 km it is 2–23°. Measuring a coating at normal incidence says almost nothing about this case.

A mirror-like face sends its glint where nobody is looking. The mirror direction of the sunlight never comes closer than 9° to any qualifying observer at 300 km (7° at 550 km). A specular coating with a narrow enough lobe therefore puts its reflection below the 20° elevation cut. The same margin limits attitude errors: tilting the face by α moves the glint by 2α, so the face tolerates only about 3.6–4.5° of tilt (550 km to 300 km), less the width of the specular lobe.

The BRDF only needs to be known over part of the hemisphere. The half-vector angle that Phong-type models depend on stays between 4° and 67°. Measurements and fits should concentrate there. The cut-offs matter. Requiring the Sun to be 18° rather than 12° down (astronomical modes) removes the overhead case at 300 km: the worst case moves to a satellite 74° up (V 3.3), and the incidence range shrinks to 73–78° from the normal. Keeping only observers at +45° (most programmes) limits the view angle at 300 km to 0–42°, against 0–64° for the +20° NEO surveys.

Inclination and the time of day the orbit crosses the equator (LTAN) don't change these peaks. They change how often each case occurs, which matters for constellation-level metrics. Solar arrays and side faces need the same mapping for their own attitude law.

5. Verification: ESA DRAMA, the Airbus–Eutelsat requirements and other tools

DRAMA (Debris Risk Assessment and Mitigation Analysis) is the free software suite ESA uses to check missions against its space debris mitigation requirements.33 Downloadable from ESA's Space Debris User Portal, sdup.esoc.esa.int/drama. The current documentation is for version 4.1.4. Version 4.1 added the parts needed for brightness: a Coefficient Estimator that meshes the 3D model, PROOF for pass and detection prediction, and a brightness analysis run mode.44 Läuferts, "Latest developments DRAMA 4", ESA, 15 January 2025.

ESA's own requirements ask constellation developers and operators to propose and implement design and operational measures that reduce visual brightness, to quantify the brightness of the design, and to share brightness data on request. The reduction requirement deliberately sets no numeric limit; ESA calls it a "seed requirement" and adds the IAU recommendation as a note. ESA names DRAMA 4.1.1 as the verification tool. Planned technology activities (from Q1 2026) add the cost of operating modes, such as slewing to avoid flares and the resulting power loss, together with coatings and materials.55 Siminski, "ESA's dark and quiet skies requirements and technology developments", ESA Space Debris Office, December 2025.

DRAMA's material data comes from Sapienza University of Rome. Its method: measure each material's BRDF in the lab, fit Blinn–Phong or build a 4D lookup table, then render the satellite as a digital twin. Checked against observed light curves of Sentinel-6A, the median error is about 0.5 magnitudes.66 Cimino, "Integrated assessment of satellite optical brightness for preservation of dark and quiet skies", Sapienza, June 2026. That is where the sizing scenario plugs in: it says which angles to measure, and DRAMA checks the whole mission against them.

Airbus Defence and Space. Under an ESA contract ("Designing Satcom missions to ensure dark and quiet skies", 4000149727/25/NL/MM/fm), Airbus and Eutelsat drafted a set of numeric, verifiable requirements, advised by IAU CPS astronomers.77 Airbus Defence & Space and Eutelsat, "Dark and Quiet Skies Requirements", technical note TN1 for ESA, public version, posted for Clean Space Days 2026. Four of its requirements map directly onto the sizing scenario:

  • REQ-DS-1, the limit: the IAU formula (V > 7 below 550 km) in the Johnson V band. Night-time means the Sun is below −12° or −18°, depending on the observing mode. The note's observing modes point telescopes at +45° and, for near-Earth-object surveys, at +30° and +20°. Section 4 uses these values.
  • REQ-DS-2, glints: no glints toward night-time observers. A glint is a rise and fall of at least 0.75 mag (twice the flux) within one second, counted between the V limit and magnitude 10 (TBC). This turns the mirror-direction margin of Table 4 into a requirement.
  • REQ-DS-3, uncertainty: magnitude predictions need a dispersion analysis covering attitude errors, geometry tolerances (including appendage angles), position errors, and BRDF measurement uncertainty, including angular sampling limitations. The sizing domain says where that sampling has to be dense.
  • REQ-DS-4, the constellation: no more than 0.32% (TBC) of the time out of specification, averaged over a year of nominal operations and a set of representative observing modes. This is where inclination and LTAN come back in, because they set how often the peak geometry occurs.

Airbus's modelling chain combines detailed geometry, BRDF-based material properties, attitude dynamics, solar-array articulation and visibility of the whole constellation. It identified the surfaces, configurations and orientations that contribute most to brightness, and evaluated surface darkening, specularity tuning, equipment relocation and solar-array orientation as mitigations.88 Lenormand (Eutelsat) and Clément (Airbus Defence & Space), "Designing Satcom Missions to Ensure Dark and Quiet Skies", Clean Space Days 2026 abstract.

6. The coupled design problem

The design choices are the facet layout (Ak, n^k), the materials (their reflectance fk and how it degrades under atomic oxygen and UV), an attitude law q(t) expressed in terms of s^, v^ and r^, and the altitude h. The objective is to maximise mission utility (for example payload aperture or capacity), or to minimise mass and cost, subject to:

Drag needs thrust (D), thrust needs power (P), and power needs solar-array area. More array area raises both brightness σ (B) and frontal area A⊥ (D). Air density varies by about an order of magnitude over the solar cycle, so this loop has to close at solar maximum. At higher altitudes, satellites dim themselves by turning their arrays edge-on to observers. In VLEO the same manoeuvre must also keep frontal area small and keep power up, which leaves far less freedom to point than at 550 km.

7. Open research questions

  1. Grazing-angle reflectance of candidate nadir-face materials (dark paints, dielectric mirror films, textured or baffled surfaces) over the sizing domain of Table 4, and how it changes under VLEO atomic-oxygen exposure. The results would extend DRAMA's small validated material set.
  2. Attitude-law optimisation: minimise the maximum σ over Gh subject to (D), (P) and (A), as an optimal-control or robust-optimisation problem across Sun–orbit geometry (orbit β-angle, season, latitude).
  3. Geometry: can other structure (skirts, a recess, array placement) shade the nadir face at grazing sunlight without increasing frontal area?
  4. Constellation-level metric: the fraction of time in Gh during which satellites exceed the limit, rather than a single satellite's worst case. REQ-DS-4 proposes 0.32% (TBC).
