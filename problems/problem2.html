Brief

The customer wants a membrane flown like a solar sail near the Sun–Earth L1 point that does the opposite of a sail: it should be almost perfectly transparent, bend 0.5 % of the sunlight passing through it away from Earth, and reflect and absorb as little as possible, because reflected and absorbed light push it the way they push a sail.

This page sets out the problem and a first design that meets it on paper. The membrane is a sheet 1.4–1.9 µm thick from tip to tip. Both faces carry sub-wavelength moth-eye cones that cancel reflection, and the back face carries a sine-shaped ripple with a 15 µm period and a few tens of nanometres deep that does the bending. Two materials work. Fused silica is the inorganic reference. Teflon AF1600 is the flexible variant the customer prefers, and it needs a 6 nm silica cap on its sun-facing cones.

Requirements

The specification is short. Table 1 lists it with the targets this design works to. Two rows needed interpretation. "3 solar constants" is a 3× dose margin at 1 AU, not a mission closer to the Sun, which also fits the −110 °C operating temperature. And "transmit 99.5 %, bend 0.5 %" cannot hold exactly while R and A are above zero, so it is read as: straight plus bent light at least 99.5 %, of which 0.5 % is bent.

What drives the design

Light that passes straight through exerts no force. Reflected light pushes with twice its momentum, absorbed light with once, and light bent by an angle θ pushes with η(1 − cos θ), which for a 1° bend is about 10⁻⁶ of the incident momentum. The drag is therefore 2R + A in units of an ideal absorber, and the bending itself is almost free. Drag pushes the membrane's balance point sunward of L1, and a shade further from Earth has to be larger to cast the same shadow, because the area needed grows with the square of the distance (Table 2).

The second driver is a coupling between mass and reflection. A free-standing film reflects strongly: bare silica at any thickness from 300 to 680 nm averages 6.6–6.8 % over 250–2500 nm and peaks near 15 %. Moth-eye cones replace the sharp surfaces with a gradual index ramp, but a ramp only works when it is long compared with the wavelength. At 2–4 µm the ramp length is set by how much material there is, so reflection falls as mass rises (Figure 1). Any dielectric has a permittivity above 1, so no material escapes this; only a lighter, lower-index material eases it.

The third driver is ultraviolet. A < 1 % only works if the material stays clear down to about 280–290 nm. That excludes every polymer with aromatic rings or carbonyl groups, which happen to be the radiation-hard ones (Table 8). The polymers that stay transparent are radiation-soft, which is why the polymer design needs an inorganic cap.

The membrane

Figure 2 shows both stacks to scale. Each is a single material through its thickness, except the polymer's cap: no coatings, no holes, nothing that expands differently with temperature.

The cone pitch must stay at or below about 170 nm. The membrane is thin enough to guide light, and a coarser lattice couples sunlight into it, which comes back out as reflection. A full electromagnetic simulation (RCWA) of the silica design shows 0.46 % reflection at 260 nm for a 250 nm pitch against 0.006 % for 150 nm, and agrees with the layered model used for everything else to within 0.03 percentage points. 11 RCWA spot checks with grcwa: at 550 nm the silica design reflects 0.093 % against 0.116 % in the layered model; at 2000 nm, 1.17 % against 1.22 %. Cone height matters: on a 1.20 g/m² silica variant, capping the cones at 300 or 450 nm raises reflection to 0.89 % or 0.45 %, against 0.37 % with free heights. The tall cones, with aspect ratios near 4 for silica and needle-like tips for AF1600, are the main fabrication challenge.

The silica core is 200 nm rather than the optically best 100 nm: breakage is the main risk, and the thicker core costs only 0.05 percentage points of reflection. The design has no holes. In glass they start cracks, and their bending effect swings strongly with wavelength. Tear-stop ribs, outside the mass limit, can be about 1 µm wide on 10 mm hexagonal cells, covering about 0.02 % of the area. Being narrow, they scatter at most about 0.04 % of the light over wide angles, which adds to the bent fraction rather than to drag.

Bending the light

The bending comes from a smooth sine-shaped variation in thickness on the back face. Its depth sets how much light is bent and its period sets the angle. A weak sine phase grating sends 99.9 % of what it bends into the ±1 orders, symmetrically, so it produces no sideways force or torque. A sine was chosen over a square profile because it has no sharp edges to couple light into the membrane. The bent fraction scales with the square of the ripple height, so ±1 nm of height gives ±10 % in bent power; uniform erosion leaves the ripple, and so the bending, unchanged.

The period follows from the geometry in Figure 3. Every bent ray must miss Earth, including rays from the far edge of the Sun reaching a membrane that sits up to one Earth radius off the Sun–Earth line. That needs 13.15 mrad, or 0.754°, at the shortest wavelength. Radiation pressure moves the balance point to about 1.57 million km, which eases this slightly to 0.732°; the design keeps the stricter number.

A membrane only shades Earth while, seen from Earth, it sits in front of the Sun's disk. At L1 that means staying within about 7,000 km of the Sun–Earth line, so the wide halo orbits used by observatories such as SOHO do not apply. Holding position inside that tube is a station-keeping problem; the 15 µm period already covers the worst offset within it.

Choice of material

Table 6 compares candidate films at the same 1.20 g/m². Low density matters more than low index, because a lighter material gives a thicker sheet and so a longer ramp. Silica is the best of the robust inorganics: transparent to about 160 nm, permeable to the hydrogen and helium the solar wind implants, and with almost no thermal expansion, so cycling strains nothing.

The customer prefers a flexible film. At this thickness, bending is not the issue: rolling a 1.4–1.9 µm sheet onto a 5 mm drum strains it by about 0.02 %, and even silica rolls to sub-millimetre radii. What a polymer adds is toughness: cracks stop instead of running, and the sheet survives punctures, creases and handling. Table 7 compares the polymers that pass the ultraviolet test of Table 8.

Teflon AF is the baseline. It is clear from about 200 nm into the infrared, has no C–H bonds to absorb near 3.4 µm, is amorphous so it does not scatter, can be cast from solution into free-standing sub-micron films as lithography pellicles are, and releases cleanly from imprint moulds. Its weakness is shared with PTFE: radiation tends to break its chains, and its tolerance to the protons that reach the whole film is the main unknown. COC is worth testing alongside it: half the density gives twice the thickness for the same mass, the toughest sheet and the lowest reflection, at the price of about 0.1 % extra absorptance from its C–H band (which holds 0.29 % of solar power) and carbonisation under protons. PVDF tends to cross-link rather than break under radiation and is the fallback if both fail, provided its crystallinity does not scatter more than 0.05 % at 1 µm. Silicones are weak at this thickness and darken under UV; PMMA breaks down under UV and radiation.

Ten years at three times the dose

The solar wind delivers the proton and helium fluences in Table 9, all stopping within a few tens of nanometres of the sun-facing surface. The anti-sun face never sees them. For silica that surface layer is too thin to matter optically: colour centres, compaction and blunted tips together change absorptance by 0.003 % (Table 10). Implanted hydrogen should escape, because at −110 °C it diffuses about 8 nm a day in silica against cone radii of 80 nm or less. Blistering at that temperature is still the open radiation risk for silica.

A bare polymer surface would not survive: the 38 J/cm² deposited in its top 50 nm would carbonise it. The 6 nm silica cap on the sun-side cones stops the protons. The cone walls lean only about 5.7° from the direction the protons travel, so each proton crosses about 60 nm of cap, twice the ~30 nm range of 1 keV hydrogen and 4 keV helium. Because of the cones' extra surface the cap weighs 0.096 g/m², the same as a flat 44 nm layer. Al₂O₃ would also block ultraviolet below about 190 nm but tends to trap helium; silica lets it escape. Either needs a plasma treatment before deposition, because ALD does not stick to a fluoropolymer, and the treatment slightly roughens and darkens the surface.

The degradation that does matter is molecular contamination. The membrane is the coldest surface around, so outgassed vapour condenses on it, and ultraviolet bakes it into an absorbing film. Every 5 nm of equivalent deposit adds about 0.07 % absorptance (Table 10). The system therefore needs a contamination budget of at most 10 nm equivalent over ten years, and no organic materials in sight of the membrane.

Temperature follows from the balance between absorbed sunlight and infrared emission from both faces. The −110 °C in the specification corresponds to absorptance about 6 % of the emissivity per side, for example A = 0.5 % with ε = 0.1, which gives −117 °C (Table 11). Outgassing is not a concern for silica; for the polymers it is mainly leftover casting solvent, and for both it is any adhesive at the mounts.

How it could be made

These routes are proposals, not demonstrated processes. For silica, a wafer-scale demonstrator (up to 300 mm) can be made by etching inverted cone pits and the ripple into a silicon wafer, filling and covering them with silica, etching the front cones, and dissolving the silicon from underneath. No process exists today for free-standing sub-micron silica at square-metre scale.

For Teflon AF, the film can be cast from solution onto a mould that carries the back cones and the ripple, the front cones imprinted from a second mould, the surface plasma-activated, the 6 nm cap grown by ALD, and the film released. Fluoropolymers release cleanly, which helps. The film would be built as tiles held by ribs, mounted slack: a thin transparent sheet bends no light when it wrinkles, so it needs no tension for flatness. Rolled stowage needs interleaf sheets or spacers so the cones of neighbouring turns do not abrade each other. A sharp crease will crack the cap along a thin line, which then darkens locally.

Risks

  1. The bulk proton dose to the polymer, from solar-particle and suprathermal protons that pass through the whole film. Its size is not yet estimated and the polymers' tolerance to it is not known.
  2. Large-area manufacture and handling of a 1.4–1.9 µm sheet carrying 160 nm-pitch cones up to 835 nm tall.
  3. Molecular contamination fixed by ultraviolet, which sets the end-of-life absorptance.
  4. Hydrogen and helium blistering at −110 °C, in the cap or in silica.
  5. Holding the ripple height to ±1 nm across the sheet.
  6. Cone damage in rolled stowage.

Verification

Real-time UV qualification is not possible: 30 sun-years is about 263,000 equivalent sun hours, so even a 10× acceleration takes three years, and the acceleration factor has to be agreed and validated. Optical properties should be measured without breaking vacuum, because air exposure partly heals radiation damage, and nanostructured surfaces take up water on the ground.

How the numbers were computed

Reflectance comes from a transfer-matrix model in which each textured layer is replaced by thin uniform slices whose index follows from the cones' fill fraction (two-dimensional Bruggeman mixing), optimised by differential evolution under the mass limit. RCWA spot checks on a hexagonal cone lattice confirm it. Silica uses the Malitson dispersion; the polymers use constant indices. The solar spectrum is a 5778 K blackbody, because the measured AM0 spectrum could not be downloaded; it overstates the ultraviolet, so the UV-driven numbers are pessimistic. Reflectance beyond 4 µm, about 1 % of solar power, is not modelled and adds an estimated 0.1 percentage points or less. Diffraction uses scalar thin-grating theory, which holds because the period is at least six times the longest wavelength. The balance point near L1 comes from the restricted three-body problem with radiation pressure. Implantation depths and the polymer surface dose are estimates, to be replaced by SRIM and environment-model runs.
